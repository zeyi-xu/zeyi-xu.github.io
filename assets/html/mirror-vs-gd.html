The reference h(x)=¼‖x‖⁴+½‖x‖² matches that growth: f is smooth and strongly convex relative to h (Lu–Freund–Nesterov, arXiv:1610.05708), which buys linear convergence. The two quartic blocks behave like a ‖·‖⁴ bowl whose curvature explodes toward the rim, so the Euclidean Hessian's largest eigenvalue is huge at the edges of the window and small in the basin — a wildly varying second-order picture that no single Lipschitz constant can honestly bound. The reference h=¼‖x‖⁴+½‖x‖² grows at the same quartic rate, and the whole story is the ratio of the two Hessians: ∇²f ⪯ L∇²h and ∇²f ⪰ μ∇²h hold with modest L, μ, so in the h-metric the condition number κ = L/μ is small even though the Euclidean one is enormous. Both constants here are measured over the window from the Hessians, not assumed: Euclidean uses the extreme eigenvalues of ∇²f; mirror uses the extreme eigenvalues of ∇²h−½ ∇²f ∇²h−½. Gradient descent steps x₊ = x − (1/L)∇f(x) with the tiny Euclidean step; mirror descent takes the same-shaped step in the dual variable — θ = ∇h(x) − (1/L)∇f(x), x₊ = (∇h)⁻¹(θ) — where ∇h(x) = (1+‖x‖²)x and its inverse is a radial Cardano solve. On the log panel the two slopes are the payoff: mirror descent falls linearly at rate (1−μ/L)ᵏ in the h-metric, while Euclidean descent's rate (1−μ/L)ᵏ is glacial because its L is inflated by the quartic corners. Drag Quartic strength up to scale A and watch the Euclidean condition number explode while the mirror one barely moves. One honest caveat: without relative strong convexity both methods are only O(1/k); the linear rate you see here comes from the ¼‖Ex‖⁴ term making f strongly convex relative to h.
